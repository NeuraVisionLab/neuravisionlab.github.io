A prediction needs a sense of its limits.

A network can produce a convincing answer even when the input is ambiguous or unlike anything it saw during training. We study how its own behavior can reveal that uncertainty—and help improve its predictions.

Our work connects stochastic prediction, iterative refinement and test-time adaptation. The papers below include current NeuraVision research and collaborative work with EPFL and other institutions, across visual recognition, segmentation and engineering prediction.

Learning from disagreement

Several predictions of the same input can expose ambiguity that a single output hides. The challenge is to obtain useful variation without training an entire collection of models.

BMVC · 2026

Stochastic nonlinearities

Instead of always applying the same activation function, individual network units randomly choose between two nonlinearities. Repeating inference with these different choices produces a family of predictions from one trained set of weights.

The mean gives the prediction; their variance provides an uncertainty estimate. Unlike dropping features, activation switching changes how information is transformed while keeping the feature pathways active. The same switching mechanism is used during training and inference.

We evaluate the approach on classification, segmentation and out-of-distribution detection, using convolutional networks and transformers. It needs no additional trainable uncertainty head, although collecting several predictions still requires multiple forward passes.

Watching a prediction settle

Some networks already revisit their answers. Their sequence of refinements contains information about which parts of the answer are reliable.

ICML · 2024

Uncertainty from iterative refinement

An iterative network receives both the original input and its previous prediction, then refines that prediction. This work finds that how quickly the outputs stabilize is closely related to prediction accuracy.

Measuring variation across successive outputs turns this behavior into a practical uncertainty signal. In road extraction, a visible road can settle quickly while a tree-covered stretch keeps changing. The same idea applies to pressure predictions on airfoils and 3D car surfaces.

The method uses an existing iterative model without adding a separate uncertainty estimator. The paper also incorporates its uncertainty into Bayesian shape optimization, helping select promising designs for further physical simulation.

Adapting when the input changes

Recognizing unreliable behavior is one step. A complementary question is whether the model can use that behavior to improve at test time.

ICML · 2025

IT³: idempotent test-time training

Idempotence means that applying a function again leaves its result unchanged. IT³ uses this idea to adapt a model to a shifted input: its prediction should remain consistent when fed back into the model.

The model is first trained with the paper’s prediction-conditioning formulation. At test time, a short optimization reduces the disagreement between successive applications, using the current input without needing its ground-truth label or a separately designed auxiliary task.

Experiments cover corrupted images, aerial-road segmentation and aerodynamic prediction. This is adaptation, rather than just an uncertainty score: the model’s parameters change during inference. The reported improvements are empirical; consistency alone cannot guarantee a correct answer on every unfamiliar input.
